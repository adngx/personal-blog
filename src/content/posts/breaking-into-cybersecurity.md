# Most important thing

Do. Your. Own. Research. You are in a different situation than me. Search for people who are already where you want to be. Ask them. Take their advice (and my advice too, you can say) with a grain of salt. Consult multiple opinions. Form some hypothesises. Then find the path / roadmap (you have to form that yourself) that **brings the highest expected value**. Then lock in. Occasionally do a reflect and be constantly on the lookout for opportunities and knowledge. (Like immerse fully in the cybersecurity world). Pivot when you need to. You will know when to pivot and when to stay, find the balance. This guide is just something I compile from my own research. I won't reinvent the wheel, do your own research.

And tbh, you can only understand what I am talking about here if you have done your own research and get a overall grasp of the industry, what jobs are available, what are the requirements, etc... (internet is free, guys)

# Understand the market

Each market is different. German market for example you can't even find a job that doesn't require bachelor's degree (even tier 1 SOC). US market is slightly looser, but for the most part you should still have a degree, mainly because everyone has one and cybersecurity is a field that people must have something to be trusted like certifications and degrees (although the real moat is still the practical experience)

This is important because I find that most advices are catered to the US. So be aware of that, you do your own research.

Personally, I don't think this is necessary, because why waste time at a job that's too basic and you would probably not good enough if you can't land something better than helpdesk??? Like I get it that I haven't even been in the job market yet, but this helpdesk advice seems to only come from US guys and seems like helpdesk is only for those wanting to jump in ASAP without any prior field knowledge and for those complete career changers i guess.... Anyways I'm focused on the German market so I can't really tell too much here, at least in my situation I would probably not have to work helpdesk (and if I have to work one I probably have too much of a skill issue).

# Certifications?

You should probably not spam them (and you are probably already broke). Security+ is just the very basic (universal opinion, you don't have to argue here). Do not think that you can do the bare minimum, pass the certification or get a degree and then immediately get a job. Each market is different and each employer has different requirements, so do your own research.

# Networking (with people, not computer networking)

By far one of the most important skills. Even if in your current area there's not much opportunities to network, try to network online. Or not even network, sending people a polite DMs asking about **their experience** (important thing here, because they won't understand your situation, and they don't have time to think about your situation) is already great. People can be surprising helpful if you actually reach out and show curiosity and be polite / know the limits (don't be a jerk) and show that **you actually have done your own research and have a overall hypothesis / grasp of the industry**. Reach out to people who are already at the place you want to be. I personally use reddit multiple times.

# Learn in public

Share what you're working on. Share what you have achieved. Share what you know. You can point people to other resources if you don't want to reinvent the wheel. Share your personal view.

Benefits:

And just don't abuse AI PLEASE!!! We don't need more AI slop (matter of fact, LinkedIn has a report button for slops). Use AI to organize your thoughts, not to invent a new slop.

# Courses

Ignore most of them because they are probably recycling existing free content or are extremely overpriced. Ignore the advices by gurus on youtube telling you to buy this course / buy this cert because most of the time they are sponsored / just following trends. Most knowledge is free guys. Use websites like Anna's Archive if you're too broke (although I recommend you at least donate to them or buy the books themselves).

Free courses are usually BS and used either for marketing or for funneling into paid things (this even applies to TryHackMe free rooms and many other things, at least in my opinion).

# Soft skills

Underrated but arguably as important as technical skills (This is again, universal opinion so don't argue with me here). Teamwork and communicating to non technical people and so on are regular parts of a cybersecurity job. Matter of fact, I am looking into Google's free technical writing course to improve my writing skills.
